§1. Why ELISENCE was born: health does not only happen in hospital

Every day, millions of people live with heart and blood-vessel disease, diabetes and blood-sugar disorders, kidney problems, digestive conditions, blood diseases and disorders, neurological and brain conditions, and different kinds of stroke.

Women are a large part of the world's population, and at different stages of life many face health issues of their own: menstrual and pelvic pain, abnormal bleeding, infections, fertility and pregnancy concerns, discomfort or pain during or after sex, and worries that sometimes go unspoken because of embarrassment, cost, distance or limited access. This list is neither complete nor meant to treat all of these as disease. Its purpose is to show that human health is continuous, many-sided and deeply human.

Every year, many people lose their lives to illness and its complications. Medicine and health systems have made remarkable progress in diagnosis, treatment and prevention, and doctors, nurses, hospitals and health services play a role nothing can replace. The problem is not that care only happens in hospital: outpatient care, family doctors, pharmacies, rehabilitation and follow-up after discharge all exist. The problem is that a person's life continues between and after these encounters, while the data, responsibilities, tools and follow-up routes do not always connect coherently.

Someone discharged from hospital today will still take medication tomorrow. They will eat, sleep and exercise, be exposed to the weather, notice changes in their symptoms, and perhaps have dozens of new questions. A chronic condition may need appropriate care and monitoring even on days without obvious pain. Poor follow-up can raise the risk of delayed attention, medication problems, confusion about the treatment plan, repeated information and, in some situations, worsening problems. The consequences are hard for the person and their family and costly for care providers and governments. Any real reduction in admissions, complications or cost must be proven with sound studies and data.

ELISENCE began with one question: why should a person's health be scattered across several systems, several apps, several appointments and several separate records, when the person has only one continuous life?

§2. What ELISENCE wants to change

ELISENCE does not claim to hold the cure for every illness. It does not replace doctors, hospitals or emergency services. Its aim is a person-centred, modular digital health ecosystem with clear governance: one understandable umbrella for services that relate to everyday life, health, care and human connection, without removing the role or independence of professional systems.

Today a person may use one app for diabetes, another for nutrition, a separate service for the weather, another for activity and weight, another system for prescriptions and appointments, and an unconnected folder for documents. Each may be good on its own, but they do not necessarily talk to each other under a shared framework of permission and standards. The person is left repeating their information or building the links themselves.

In the ELISENCE vision, the experience is organised around the person rather than around one app or one disease. Related services can share the information another authorised part needs, for a defined purpose — where there is a real connection, valid consent, a trustworthy source, a genuine need and appropriate authorisation. This does not mean every part can see every piece of information, and it does not guarantee that every connection is active in the product today. The core idea is simple: the right information, for the right purpose, to the right role, at the right time.

Example 1 — Diabetes, diet and weather

Imagine a person with diabetes who follows a nutrition plan for their weight and blood sugar and also lives with changing environmental conditions. A standalone diabetes app may see only the recorded glucose; a diet app only meals and calories; a weather app only temperature or pollution. ELISENCE aims to bring this information together in one orderly experience, wherever it is real and permitted: recorded blood glucose, meals, calorie and water intake, activity and environmental conditions. The intended result is better awareness and sharper questions for the care team. It is not to infer automatically the cause of a problem, a need to change medication, or a definitive clinical conclusion.

If water intake, calories, activity, sleep and weight history are added, the person can better understand their habits and changes over time. Adjusting a treatment plan or medication still depends on professional judgement and sound evidence.

Example 2 — Diabetes alongside blood, kidney, heart and brain conditions

A person with diabetes may also have kidney problems, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or cardiovascular or neurological issues. In a shared ecosystem, permitted data and observations can be seen side by side, improving the longitudinal picture and the conversation with the clinician. The related modules — Blood/Lab, Medical Tracking, Medication and Predictive Health — must keep their boundaries: recording and non-diagnostic explanation of a result, calculating or displaying a risk pattern, and a professional decision about diagnosis and treatment are not the same thing. Where heart attack or stroke is concerned, no general-purpose system should present itself as a substitute for emergency care.

Example 3 — Women's health alongside other conditions

Imagine a woman managing diabetes and abnormal blood results who also has period pain, pelvic pain or unusual bleeding. An app limited to the menstrual cycle may know nothing about her other conditions. ELISENCE wants to bring together the information she has permitted and actually recorded, in an orderly experience, so that better questions, non-diagnostic guidance and timely referral to a specialist become possible. The presence of diabetes or a blood test cannot be taken to mean the cause of her pain has been found. Severe pain, worrying bleeding, fever or other warning signs must be directed to proper medical assessment.

Example 4 — A healthy person living more healthily

ELISENCE is not only for people who are ill. Someone with no known condition may want to manage their weight, record what they eat, drink enough water, understand their activity and sleep, and follow changes in their body. For weight-management treatments such as GLP-1 medicines, recording progress and educational questions can sit under the supervision and advice of the prescriber. ELISENCE must not prescribe medication or suggest dose changes on its own.

§3. Health over time: from scattered reports to a person's understandable story

Imagine a patient who has had several blood tests over a year. A general tool can explain one test, or even two files, but the ELISENCE experience aims to go further than a one-off answer. It keeps reports in order with their dates, source, units and reference ranges, alongside recorded medicines, meals, activity and the person's own notes, so changes can be seen over time. Within the boundary of authorised records retrieved from their source, ELISA can say in plain language how many tests are on file, how a marker such as cholesterol has changed on specific dates, and which questions might be worth raising with the doctor.

A change in a test marker does not by itself prove that a diet, a medicine or any particular action caused it. The device, test method, units, timing and clinical circumstances all matter, and every explanation should state its confidence and limits. Structured information and documents in Documents and Medical Journey can be available to the person and to authorised people, without all data automatically flowing to every service or every person.

§4. Who is ELISA? Not just another chatbot

ELISA, the intelligent companion of ELISENCE, is meant to stay alongside the person: warm, respectful and easy to understand, and attentive to their permissions, preferences and the real course of their life. ELISENCE is designed so that users do not feel they are facing a cold list of programs. At home, on the move, while logging a meal or getting ready for a doctor's visit, the person should be able to ask in natural speech: "When is my next appointment?", "Which medicines are on my record?", "What did the doctor talk about at my last visit?", "How do I understand my test result?", "Can you open the right part of the app?"

What should set ELISA apart from a general chatbot is that, within the bounds of connected and authorised sources and services, she can be a companion with a defined, traceable context. This does not mean ELISA has every capability of ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude today, or that all of a person's memory and medical information is always and unconditionally available to her. Warmth must never turn into claims of knowing someone's feelings for certain, or into unhealthy dependence.

Medication reminders and voice interaction

In the voice vision, and with the user's prior set-up, ELISA might say: "Mary, it's time for your recorded medicine." Mary answers, "I've taken it," and, after checking identity and that the command matches the right schedule, the system updates the record. The process must also handle confirmed status, mishearing, cancellation and correction. Recording the user's answer is not physical proof that the medicine was taken.

Environment and wellbeing

If a person permits the use of their health characteristics and local weather data, ELISA could, in a future design, offer alerts in line with policy and sound evidence. Air pollution matters for some conditions, but an alert must not be issued on general assumptions without a medical source and validation. Knowing the person's country, city or weather also depends on a reliable location source, consent and a data connection.

Carers, family and asking for help

ELISENCE is designed to let a person name in advance the people they trust: family, a carer, a doctor and, where possible, emergency services. When the person needs help, ELISA and the ELISENCE interface can make choosing a contact and asking for help simpler. In an advanced design, no response to important reminders, or certain risk situations, could lead to an escalation workflow the person has agreed to in advance. But activating and sending a real alert to people or emergency services requires contracts, security, connectivity, testing and independent reliability. Silence after a reminder is not necessarily a medical incident.

The founder has shown four new screenshots: the Home screen and the path "Who do you want to alert?" → Carer → Hold to Send → "Help request prepared". The last screen itself states: "No real alert was sent in this test build." These images therefore prove that an interactive interface exists in a test build. They are not evidence of a real call to a carer, family, ambulance or emergency service. The Founder confirms the demonstration account is a test account and its visible avatars are AI-generated, not photographs of real individuals. Separate review remains appropriate for any other actual personal information, non-avatar images, source rights or third-party content before public distribution.

Tiredness, loneliness and calm

Sometimes a person is simply tired, anxious, upset or lonely. ELISA can listen respectfully, offer general, non-diagnostic support and, where warning signs appear, suggest professional or emergency help. Human companionship is the aim of the experience, not a promise to treat mental illness or replace specialist care.

§5. Medicines, tests, Documents and a traceable experience

Any user may store blood tests, ultrasound reports, doctors' letters, prescriptions, medication lists, care instructions, visit reports, clinic results or family members' documents in Documents, organised by category and date. A document may be an image or a PDF. Extracting information from it, recognising text correctly, matching names and dates, protecting the source and permitting another person to see it are all separate steps.

In future, the user should be able to say to ELISA: "Find my blood test from six months ago," "Compare this test with the previous one," "What was recommended at my last visit?" ELISA must quote and conclude only from documents that actually exist and are authorised. She must make clear which part is quoted from a document and which is general explanation, and must not invent an answer when something is not on file or she is unsure. ELISA's access to visit transcripts, prescriptions or test results depends on lawful storage and valid access.

A user may let a carer, doctor or family member see only specific documents for a set period. That sharing must be visible, limited, revocable and logged — never a free transfer of a whole health history to anyone.

§6. Appointments, Family Doctor and the care journey in practice

ELISENCE's connected care model starts at home. From the same place, the person can send an appointment request to a GP, a specialist, a hospital, a nurse, a midwife or another authorised centre, choosing a preferred date and time. But an appointment request is not the same as a confirmed booking: the provider must confirm its capacity, connection and authority. Some visits will be in person, others by phone or video.

In the intended video-visit workflow, the doctor can bring another member of the care team into the session, with appropriate access and consent. Where permitted, the patient can also invite a carer or family member. Being in the session does not grant access to the whole record. The session owner, the patient and every team member must know who receives sound and video, which document is visible, who may speak or receive files, and when access ends. That transparency matters even more for older people and people who need support.

The older father

Imagine an older father who talks with his doctor but cannot remember all the advice afterwards. With consent and an appropriate legal basis, the system could, in a future design, record or transcribe the conversation and place a reviewable summary in Documents. Later, the patient or an authorised carer could ask ELISA, "What did we talk about at last week's visit?" or "What was the recorded instruction for the medicine?" A transcript may contain errors, so important medication information must be checked against the prescription and what the doctor confirmed. Some jurisdictions require the consent of all participants, formal notice and a retention policy.

Prescriptions, pharmacy and potential savings

ELISENCE's professional-care workflow is designed so that a qualified doctor can prepare a structured prescription as part of their work, issue it where legal authorisation exists, and let the patient or an authorised pharmacy see its status. Where a real connection and contract exist, the pharmacy could prepare the order for collection or delivery. This could reduce travel, waiting time and repeated work; no saving in energy, fuel, labour or cost is claimed without real data and authorised operation. A draft prescription or a medicine displayed in the app is not a legally issued prescription or a working pharmacy connection.

What Medical Intelligence does

Medical Intelligence is not just a drug encyclopaedia. It is a specialist information layer that helps the user and the authorised doctor understand a medicine's identity, active ingredients, dosage form, manufacturer, reliable reference sources, country information and related information options. A medicine with the same active ingredient or apparent function is not necessarily interchangeable for the same patient. Decisions about substitution, interactions, dose changes and prescriptions belong to a qualified doctor or pharmacist. National drug references such as NHS dm+d, and their equivalents in other countries, must be checked for data licence, reliability and currency.

Changing doctor and keeping the record

If a person changes doctor, they should not have to rebuild their whole story from scratch. ELISENCE wants to make it simpler to pass on relevant context through an orderly history, traceable documents and authorised sharing. The new doctor can see only information made available with valid consent, care authority and a real connection to the source. "Every record from every hospital in the world, on your phone" is a false promise; moving towards a clear, portable record that belongs to the person is a goal worth stating.

§7. Medical Tracking, GPS and the safety of vulnerable people

For a person with a chronic illness, older age, memory problems, Alzheimer's disease or any condition where getting lost or forgetting medication creates risk, ELISENCE's design includes a set of configurable features: care appointments, medication times, chosen routes and places, trusted contacts, help requests and family support.

In future, rules could be defined that, with appropriate consent and tested reliability, inform a carer of a particular situation. For example, the person starts a help request themselves, or an authorised workflow checks a missed confirmation request. A missed response must never on its own be treated as an emergency or as the person being lost. Location must be used only for that exact purpose and only for as long as needed. Any active emergency service needs an agreement with the provider, network access, transparency about possible failure, and end-to-end testing. In a real crisis, an app cannot replace the official emergency number.

This example is exceptionally powerful for showing ELISENCE's human value. In the Investor Pitch, however, a tested help-request interface must be kept clearly separate from real alert delivery and deployment-ready emergency services.

§8. Zero Trust: how information is protected

The more useful and connected a person's information becomes, the more its protection matters. The founder emphasises Zero Trust: no device, account, request, program or person is trusted without limit simply because it is inside the system. For every important decision, the system must know who is asking, on whose behalf, in what role and with what permission, for what purpose, from which source and for how long.

In plain words: - Identity: the user's account and session are checked. Authentication is more than having a username. - Role: an ordinary person, a carer, a doctor, a nurse, a hospital manager, a researcher and a government body do not have the same powers. - Whose data: the account holder may be a parent or carer, but a health record belongs to a specific person. Access to an account does not automatically grant access to every family member's records. - Purpose and consent: access to a document for clinical care differs from research use, social posting or marketing. Consent and legal basis must match the use. - Minimisation: only the information needed should move. The weather service has no need to see a blood test. - Origin and integrity: it is known where each record came from, when it was recorded and whether it has changed. Important events are written to an audit log. - Control and revocation: as far as possible, the user and authorised parties should be able to see, limit or revoke active access. Some legal retention duties apply regardless of withdrawn consent. - Technical security: the design may use encryption in transit and at rest, key and session management, auditing, access restriction and closed-by-default gates. The exact level of implementation must be confirmed from real documents and tests.

No technology is "100% unhackable", and no system should claim that impersonation is "completely impossible". The professional standard is provable controls, risk reduction, error detection and accountability.

§9. Two kinds of registration, professionals and children's accounts

Personal account

An ordinary user signs up by controlling an email address or phone number, establishing a session identity and creating a personal account. They then add a profile, username, preferences and the information they choose to provide. The real verification steps for phone, email and secure sign-in must be described exactly as the current app does them; no general claim that every method is complete is valid without proper testing.

Parent, child and carer

The design rules provide for an adult account and dependent accounts for children or people under guardianship. A parent's or guardian's access must reflect age, legal authority, appropriate consent and whose data it is. A child's photo or avatar in a family account is a personal feature, but showing a child's image in a public presentation needs separate permission. Changes to the profile, consent management and the end of a guardian's authority must be designed into the architecture.

Professionals

A professional cannot become a doctor simply by choosing the option "Doctor". Professional registration must be a separate stage: identity verification, checking qualifications and professional credentials against a reliable source, matching role and organisation, authority to work on records, and geographic or jurisdictional limits. The founder mentions Veriff for document and face verification, but naming Veriff in the design does not prove that every contractual and operational step is complete today. Even face verification alone is not a licence to practise or to access a patient.

§10. How information is shared

The patient decides what kind of sharing to request: last week's test result with a specific doctor, an appointment with a carer, or a particular document with a pharmacist. Before showing anything, the system must check the recipient's identity and role, the care relationship, the country and source of the record, the purpose, the expiry time and the consent policy. Some information may not be shareable for legal or clinical reasons, or the connection to that system may not exist yet. Important events must be auditable.

Sharing on the ELIS social network or sending a file to a friend is not the same as sharing a medical record between two doctors. Private health information must never move by default into a public post or commercial research. Sharing for research and government follows a separate route; receiving a statistical report is not permission to see identifiable data about individuals.

§11. Eight languages and region-awareness in practice

ELISENCE is designed for eight languages on selected surfaces: English (EN), Persian (FA), Arabic (AR), Turkish (TR), Romanian (RO), Albanian (SQ), Spanish (ES) and Greek (EL). Persian and Arabic need correct right-to-left display. Adding further languages is a development goal. An eight-language architecture does not mean every app capability, document and clinical pathway is active and validated in all eight languages.

Region-aware means the experience and the policies of each jurisdiction are aligned: language, medicine sources, consent laws, doctors' credentials, privacy, service names and permission to use specialist modules. If a user travels from Romania to the UK, current location is only one signal; residence, professional identity, where the treating source is, and the provider's authority also matter. It cannot be said that every connection from Romania "automatically falls under MDR" or that every connection from the UK "connects to the NHS". The NHS is a service-delivery system and source of resources; the FDA is the US regulator; EU MDR is the medical-device framework; the GCC is a group of countries with different requirements. None of them means official approval or a live connection just because software knows where it is. IFDA may only be referred to within legal and permitted bounds, with source and country confirmed.

Internal knowledge sources, including topic libraries on women's health and medicine references built on reliable sources, can reduce dependence on outside sources for part of the experience. But they need versioning, licences, scientific review, updating and a clear line from diagnosis. Take pelvic pain: ELISA can explain general information and warning signs in plain language and, based on authorised records, suggest questions to take to an appointment. She does not diagnose the cause of pain from a few data points.

§12. Fair access, women's health and underserved communities

ELISENCE is built to support a girl reaching puberty, a woman living in a remote area, a person for whom cost and distance make visits difficult, and a woman who feels ashamed to ask about painful sex or bleeding. A private, multilingual, understandable and safe space can make learning, and being ready to seek help, easier. ELISENCE's aim is that basic health information and trustworthy non-diagnostic guidance should depend as little as possible on wealth, geography or the courage to ask.

This vision must not turn into promises that "all illness will be prevented", that "treatment is available from anywhere in the world", or that "millions of lives have been saved". The difference between the aspiration to save lives and a proven clinical outcome must stay clear in every presentation. Possible social value and cost reduction for governments need evaluation in independent pilots.

§13. ELIS — why the heart of ELISENCE is a social, human experience

If care only remembers a person when they are ill, many people have no reason to stay in an app while they feel well. This principle has guided ELISENCE from the start: nobody wants their identity to be only "patient", and nobody wants to live their ordinary life every day in a place that feels like a hospital.

That is why ELISENCE puts a space for human interaction at the centre of the experience: ELIS. It is meant as a place to connect with friends and family, follow people and pages of interest, talk, join groups and share life experiences. A person can raise topics such as exercise, food, learning, self-care, a recovery journey or daily events with the audience they choose. The user controls the subject of every post; their medical history is never published on the social network by default.

ELISENCE's wider design for this space includes: - personal profile, username and optional avatar - friends and followers - Pulse and other post and story types - photos, video, text, comments and reactions - one-to-one and group conversations - voice and video calls - personal video channels; recording, editing and publishing content - sharing articles or audio files - support and exercise groups; communities with shared goals - birthday reminders and digital gifts - media management and in-app file download

These make up the founder's complete vision, and they must not all be presented at once as "working in the released product". Tested capabilities, such as parts of Pulse, must be named according to development receipts and independent evidence.

Goals, positive motivation and healthy living

Instead of relying on fear of illness or addiction to notifications, ELISENCE wants to support small, understandable progress: a week of regular activity, attention to a sleep routine, logging food, reaching a goal the person set themselves, or joining others in healthy habits. Under Achievements and Rewards, digital achievements and rewards can be defined for suitable goals, and the user may share them if they wish. "Positive dopamine" in the founder's text is a metaphor for healthy motivational design, not a proven neuroscientific or therapeutic claim.

Support groups can create a sense of companionship, but other people's experience is not medical advice — especially for medicines, warning symptoms, treatment plans, pregnancy and private matters.

How ELIS and health connect

ELIS is not meant to hand medical data to a social network. Instead, the user can be the same person who talks with friends today and, in the private part of the app, goes to the right service for medicine, weight or an appointment, each with its own access controls. This is the integration of a human experience, not the unrestricted merging of private data.

§14. ELISENCE Media — education, creativity and independent content

ELISENCE Media is the distinct media and publishing part of the ecosystem and must not be confused with ELIS or ELISA. ELISENCE is designed to let an authorised user or creator organise educational videos, suitable reports, health-related content and creative content in formats such as Media Studio, Playlists and channels: creating or uploading, categorising, previewing, managing playlists and publishing to a suitable audience.

Ideally, someone with a question about nutrition, women's health, exercise or an app feature can find clear, understandable content. Advertising or sponsored content, if it is ever permitted and defined, must be kept separate from independent education, clearly labelled, subject to data-protection rules and independent of any sale of health records. ELISENCE's revenue is not built on selling personal medical information or on advertising targeted with health data.

Media Studio and Playlists have real images in FC2. Those images do not mean that every recording, editing, publishing, payment, advertising and playback feature is active in every country. Labels such as "Coming Soon" must remain in any real demonstration.

§15. A guide video on every screen — a learning aim not yet built

ELISENCE's user-experience and accessibility plan calls for a video-guide button on every important screen. That way the user can, if they wish, see how the screen works: how to enter information, how to save a document, how to grant sharing permission, or how to use ELISA's guidance. This feature is not yet installed on any screen and must be presented as a user-experience and accessibility plan. For older or less experienced users, or people with different language needs, short video guides can help. They do not replace a simple, accessible interface.

§16. ELISENCE Universe — five gateways to life, health and connection

From the home screen, choosing ELISENCE Universe takes the user into visual, multi-domain navigation. Each domain is a doorway to a family of services. These five domains must not be confused with the six "continuity functions" in the Investor Pitch: the five domains describe where the user goes, while the six functions describe the role the ecosystem's architecture plays. Both differ again from the authority rings.

Domain 1 — Medical & Clinical

Designed to organise the care journey and personal and professional information. The founder's examples: - Medical Journey - Medical Tracking - Blood/Lab - Appointments - Medication - Family Doctor - Documents - Clinical Profile - Prescription - professional communications

The person can organise their history, documents and requests better. A doctor or hospital can use dedicated tools where there is a separate connection and authorisation. Not every service is active in every country, and the name "Clinical" does not mean the product holds medical authorisation for public use.

Domain 2 — Health Intelligence

Covers analysis, trend observation, health knowledge, authorised context and certain specialist engines. Examples: - views of laboratory changes and longitudinal charts - diabetes and glucose data - cardiovascular status - neurological and brain status - risk patterns - Blood/Lab Intelligence, in its proper place

Predictive Health belongs to Phase 8, with its own advanced and separate governance gates; Medical Intelligence, GP, Prescription and Medical Tracking belong to Phase 3. Showing an algorithm, or a card marked "Contract only", does not prove that an active clinical diagnostic tool exists. The user must know which data was observed, which interpretation was calculated, and which decision belongs to the doctor.

Domain 3 — Nutrition & Metabolism

Very concrete for everyday life: - weight, meals, calories and food composition - water intake - activity and sleep - managing weight trends and food history

In a testable flow, the user photographs a meal, reviews the suggested identification, corrects or accepts it and saves it, then can return to the history. If confirmed with a valid test receipt, this flow is a far better example for an investor than claiming live integration with every hospital. For GLP-1, the system can support recording and related education, but changing treatment belongs to the specialist.

Domain 4 — Life & Care

In this space a person is not just "a patient". It can include: - women's and girls' health at different life stages - the menstrual cycle, and information on pain and general symptoms - family care, parent/guardian and child - ageing - calm and non-diagnostic mental wellbeing - behavioural and lifestyle topics

Sensitive groups such as children, pregnant women or people with serious illness should get safe general guidance, understandable information and a route to a doctor — not automatic diagnosis or unauthorised sharing with family members.

Domain 5 — Connections & Services

This is where the user's links with friends, family, carers, community, trusted people, communications and authorised services live. ELIS, ELISENCE Media, communication features and support-request routes can all be part of the overall experience. But creating a social profile does not grant professional access or membership of an official care network. For a doctor or institution to take part, identity verification and specific rules are required.

§17. Body Scan — watching body changes, not diagnosing from a picture

Body Scan is part of ELISENCE's approach to body monitoring and weight management. Done properly, the person follows step-by-step guidance to capture suitable images of the body from set angles. Light, distance, posture, clothing, height or scale, and the device are checked. A model might then detect body landmarks, segment the image and estimate geometry, suggesting some approximate measurements or trends over time. Results must come with a confidence level, a margin of error and the ability to reject an unsuitable image.

An ordinary photo cannot determine body-fat percentage, a medical condition or every precise measurement with certainty. Even with LiDAR or other aids, validation across different devices and bodies is required. Body images are highly sensitive, so their processing, storage and sharing need separate controls.

§18. Sensors — affordable sensing and manual entry side by side

ELISENCE is designed to make health-sensing technology more accessible, not only to the wealthy. In the ELISENCE model, a person can record their daily data even without a sensor. Where a suitable device and an authorised connection exist, sensor data can be added with labels for source, time, quality and device. The design aim is cheaper sensors and wearables that work with the services.

But "designing a low-cost sensor" does not mean a mass-produced hardware product, a fixed retail price, medical-device approval or reliable measurement for every illness. The technical path can be explained as sensor → signal → context → authorised analysis → safety check → longitudinal display → human decision. If a source drops out, the app must show that data is missing rather than invent a number. Spoken entry and voice confirmation must remain usable independently of any sensor.

§19. Phase 4 — global infrastructure for trust, provenance, exchange and institutional analysis

A central question for ELISENCE is: if it can organise authorised, high-quality health context in a person's life, is this architecture only useful to one user? The answer in the Phase 4 vision is that a layer for institutional trust, data quality and provenance control, standard exchange and auditable analysis could also serve hospitals, universities, health networks and governments.

In earlier documents, Phase 4 has been described through topics such as: - the Global Health Trust & Exchange Layer - Global Analytics & Evidence - Provenance & Integrity - institutional infrastructure

Its role is not to sell a database of patients. Its role is to provide a mechanism through which authorised, validated, purpose-appropriate data can be used while the source owner keeps its rights and responsibilities. References to FHIR, health exchange, research models such as OMOP, national policy, signatures and hashes, audit, and role- or attribute-based authorisation all need specific design and implementation evidence. An architecture document or an API test is not confirmation of national deployment.

A hospital

A hospital could use a post-discharge follow-up tool for patients under its own care, in a separate, contracted deployment. Rather than seeing a list of everyone on ELISENCE, the organisation wants to know: - which records within its own authorised pathway need follow-up - whether a message reached the patient by an approved route - whether a follow-up appointment has been booked - whether there is unnecessary duplication in administrative work

This data must come from real systems with the right permission. Possible results — less repeated information, better follow-up completion or staff time saved — must be measured in a pilot.

A government and ministry of health

In a future design, a ministry may want to analyse general population-health patterns within the law and its authorisation. That could include patterns of attendance and service coverage, regional needs, differences in access, or reliable aggregate indicators for planning. A minister or government official must never, by default, see the names, phone numbers, medicines or medical records of all users. In the protective model, outputs are produced as far as possible in aggregate form, with: - minimum group-size controls - query limits - removal of identifiable combinations

In some ELISENCE designs, a K-anonymity threshold of 50 has been reported: when a group is smaller than the threshold, the response is insufficient_data. This rule must be confirmed from source and tests for each route, and on its own it is not enough to remove every re-identification risk.

A university or research institute

A researcher studying population trends, health habits or the possible effect of an intervention may request appropriately protected or de-identified data, or aggregate statistics. The research topic, ethics approval, legal basis, data conditions, purpose of processing, ban on re-identification and retention period must all be defined. Research access to all identifiable records is never created just by opening an ordinary account. In some scenarios, even pseudonymised data can count as personal data and need legal protection.

A pharmaceutical company or insurer

A pharmaceutical company might have questions about population-level treatment needs, product safety or authorised scientific research. An insurer might want to fund an agreed service for eligible members. These uses do not mean buying or selling individuals' medical records, disease-based advertising or unlimited commercial access. Contracts, data rights, privacy, conflict-of-interest policy and the ban on selling health records must stay clear. ELISENCE can sell a service model and software licence, not ownership of patients' private information.

Why Phase 4 matters to an investor

Because the architecture goes beyond a consumer interface and opens a modular, auditable, authorised B2B/B2G model: - a hospital licenses one module for a defined pathway - an organisation licenses a selected suite - a suitable network licenses a larger package

This is a commercial opportunity, not a claim that nationwide contracts have been signed today. Phase 4 must be presented with the real state of the code and its security and deployment-readiness gates.

§20. Phase 8 — health intelligence, risk patterns and longitudinal understanding under human authority

In this story, Phase 8 links areas within their own specialist boundaries: - Blood/Lab Intelligence - risk analysis - observing patterns over time and multi-dimensional charts - blood-glucose context - heart and blood vessels, brain and nerves, stroke - the environment and other factors

Parts of it may be engineered or tested, but public release and clinical validity are assessed separately for each capability.

For a person

If a person has recorded glucose and activity, their blood tests are validly stored in Documents and their medicines are correctly recorded, longitudinal charts can show which measurements exist and when. ELISA can ask simple questions to help them understand these trends, or give non-diagnostic, sourced explanations. Showing a "risk bucket" or a worrying sign is not a medical diagnosis or a definite prediction of stroke. When data is incomplete, the output must show missing data or uncertainty.

For a doctor

In a separate environment and with valid authorisation, a doctor may be able to review their own patient's recorded trends, the data sources, the time window and an algorithm's explanatory reasons. Professional interpretation, clinical decisions and prescriptions remain with the doctor. Even a powerful predictive system can be wrong when its data is wrong, biased, or outside the range it was trained on. That is why events, input quality and provenance, algorithm version rules and uncertainty are all needed.

For a hospital

In a sound pilot, a hospital may want to evaluate: - the number of reviewable alerts - follow-up trends - the workload a service creates

Sensitivity, specificity, false alarms, effect on staff and the real outcome must be measured. ELISENCE must not say that because a risk engine has been built, a hospital has already reduced strokes or admissions.

For public health, universities and research

In future, Phase 8 could provide appropriate, authorised outputs for group analysis or research, but access to them must be tied to Phase 4 controls and to data policy and law. No researcher or minister can extract individual records from Phase 8 engines simply because of an organisational role. Population outputs require minimum group sizes, re-identification protection and request logging.

How Phase 8 relates to Phase 3

Phase 3 covers Medical Intelligence, GP, Prescription, Medical Tracking and the services of the care journey. Phase 8 covers a set of advanced intelligence, risk and pattern analysis, with separate activation and boundaries. Phase 4 covers exchange, provenance, trust and institutional analysis. These three must never be fused into a single page or name that leads an investor to think they are all one released medical product.

§22. Commercial and investment logic — without turning the patient into a product

The founder built ELISENCE with his own capital and in-house work, on a developed architecture. An investor now needs to know that the product is not just a multi-part idea; it also has a credible commercial logic.

R6G defines six payer pathways: 1. governments 2. healthcare organisations 3. insurers 4. employers 5. enterprises 6. consumers

There are three offer formats: Single Module, Selected Suite and Whole-Platform Licence.

The commercial safeguards: - Basic services already paid for through one route are not charged to the patient again. - Premium is optional and needs explicit consent. - Personal health information is not sold. - Price and delivery capacity are set by country and contract.

In plain words: - A hospital might pay for a specific module for authorised post-discharge follow-up or for organising care communications. - A public body might use an authorised infrastructure or analytics service with measurable goals. - An employer or insurer might fund a voluntary, proportionate service for eligible people — without free access to their health records. - A person might buy some uncovered features, only by their own explicit choice.

A proposed pilot is not a confirmed sale or a signed contract. Real economic value must be measured with criteria such as follow-up completion, staff time, repeated data entry and cost of service. Investment here is meant to accelerate: - building a multidisciplinary team - validation - compliance for the intended use - authorised integrations - market entry - scale

It is not meant to buy outside services in place of the technology the ELISENCE team builds in-house. The amount of capital raised, the valuation and any private dilution limit must not appear in public text without a specific decision by the founder.

§23. The future of ELISENCE — from daily companion to institutional partnership

ELISENCE is not a set of apps side by side; it is one continuous path with the person at its centre. One person can talk with a friend, log their food, reach a health goal, ask ELISA about a document, share valid information with an authorised doctor and, after a visit, see their history in order. The doctor and the hospital each keep their own professional authority. Government or researchers, instead of watching everyone's private life, have access only to authorised, protected outputs with a defined purpose.

With this approach, health becomes part of ordinary life, not just the consulting room. The person can feel more accompanied and more aware. With correct deployment, the doctor can have better context for decisions. The care organisation can test the quality of follow-up and the value of services in real pilots. And new opportunities can open up for access in remote and underserved areas.

This is a large aim, and it demands honesty: the final vision, the architecture built, the tests carried out, the active services, real institutional connection and clinical authorisation are five different things. ELISENCE's greatness comes from combining this vision with its step-by-step delivery, not from claiming that every promise is already real today.
