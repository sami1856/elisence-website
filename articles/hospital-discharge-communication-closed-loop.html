After Discharge, Who Holds the Thread?

Why a hospital handover is more than a document

By Sam Asi — Founder & CEO of ELISENCE · 5 November 2026

A person can leave hospital with a discharge letter in hand and still be uncertain about what happens next.

Which medicines have changed? Who will arrange the follow-up? What should happen if a new symptom appears? Has the next care team received the information it needs? These are ordinary questions at a vulnerable moment. They reveal a gap that is easy to miss when we judge a handover only by whether a document was produced or sent.

A handover is not complete merely because information has moved. Someone must be able to receive it, understand it, take responsibility for the next step and know how the patient can ask for help.

1. The last mile of a care transition

In England, NICE guidance sets out practical expectations for discharge planning. It recommends a named coordinator, communication between hospital and community teams, a discharge summary for the GP within 24 hours, and a copy for the patient on the day of discharge. It also calls for follow-up arrangements and clear contact details where needed.[1]

Government guidance makes the transition wider than a document exchange. Relevant information should reach the person and the services providing onward care in a timely, accurate way. That can include medication changes, communication needs, care preferences and the support expected from carers or family.[2]

These expectations point to four separate questions:

  1. Was the information sent?
  2. Did the right person or team receive it?
  3. Can the patient and carer understand the plan?
  4. Is it clear who owns the next action, and when it should happen?

If we measure only the first, we may confuse transmission with continuity. A record can be accurate while the next appointment remains unclear. A medicine list can exist while the person is unsure which version to follow. A referral can be made while nobody knows whether it was accepted.

This is not an accusation that clinicians do not care. Discharge involves busy teams, different services, changing responsibilities and real capacity constraints. It is a design question: how can systems make the next step visible and accountable to the people who need it?

2. What the evidence says — and what it does not

The issue is documented in current policy and patient-experience research. A 2026 Healthwatch Leicester and Leicestershire report drew on the views of 119 people. Its summary describes some improvement in how involved people felt compared with earlier local research, while identifying continuing challenges around communication, access to medication and support for carers.[3] Those findings matter, but they are local evidence; they should not be presented as a national estimate.

Research also suggests that communication can affect outcomes. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in JAMA Network Open included 60 trials in its qualitative review and pooled 19 randomised trials involving 3,953 patients for readmission. Across the interventions studied, improved discharge communication was associated with lower readmission, higher treatment adherence and greater patient satisfaction.[4]

That is meaningful evidence for taking communication seriously. It is not proof that every readmission can be prevented, that one intervention works for every person, or that a digital platform will produce the same results. The interventions varied, and care transitions depend on far more than information alone.

The careful conclusion is narrower: communication is part of the safety and experience of a transition, and better-designed communication deserves attention alongside staffing, clinical judgement and service capacity.

3. From “sent” to “closed loop”

A useful next step is to design for confirmation, not just dispatch. For each important transition, the system should make it possible to see:

  • the current plan the patient has been given;
  • the medicines and changes that need reconciliation;
  • the named service or person responsible for the next action;
  • whether the receiving team has acknowledged the handover;
  • a clear route for the patient or carer to ask a question.

This does not mean placing the burden on patients to chase every organisation. It means giving patients a readable view of their own next steps while making responsibility between services explicit. NHS England’s model discharge pathway similarly centres whether people understand their plan, medicines, follow-up and route to support.[5]

Digital tools can help keep that view together over time, but they cannot replace a clinician, repair missing service capacity or guarantee that separate organisations share data. They must work within consent, privacy and clear safety boundaries. The design goal should be to help people orient themselves and help teams see what remains unresolved.

4. Why this matters to ELISENCE

ELISENCE is a founder-funded, UK-based modular digital-health ecosystem under active development. It was conceived around the difficulty people face when health information and support are spread across different moments and services. Its intelligent companion, ELISA, is part of a broader design vision for helping people make sense of health information and routines over time.

That is a product direction, not a claim that ELISENCE is integrated with NHS discharge systems or has demonstrated clinical outcomes. Any future role in care transitions would need appropriate partnerships, governance, validation and a clearly defined scope.

The question is larger than whether a discharge letter exists. It is whether the person leaving hospital, the receiving service and the people supporting them can all see the next step — and know who is responsible for it.
